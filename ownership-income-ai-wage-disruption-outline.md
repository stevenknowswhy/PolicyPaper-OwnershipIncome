## Working title

**Employee Ownership Income: Forms, Trade-offs, and Its Place in an AI Economy**

The earlier title, *Who Owns the Machine*, names the larger project. This paper is the employee-ownership piece of that project.

## One-paragraph contribution

This paper asks how employee ownership income can be widened now, through the way firms are structured, and what that layer can and cannot do as AI shifts income from wages toward capital. It compares the forms — employee stock ownership plans, worker cooperatives, employee ownership trusts, broad-based equity, profit sharing, and revenue sharing — on what the employee actually holds, who is included, when cash arrives, and what risk sits with the job. The return comes from the company's earnings and the employee's claim on that company. Public policy can make those structures easier for businesses to adopt. It is not the source of the income. The five-layer floor in the thesis below is the setting. This paper does not design the funds or the universal basic income. It places employee ownership inside that future and says what policymakers can do today.

## Author's overarching thesis

Recorded 2026-10-20 from the author. This is the architecture the paper examines and, if the evidence supports it, recommends. It is the claim under test. It is not yet a finding.

**The architecture.** A citizen's baseline is built from five layers, fitted to the country, the state, and the community where they live:

1. A federal sovereign wealth fund.
2. A state sovereign wealth fund.
3. A community sovereign wealth fund.
4. Employee ownership income.
5. A universal basic income.

Together these layers are the floor. No citizen falls beneath the baseline they produce. The floor is a mixture of cash, credits, and subsidies, and it is what people use to pay for the necessities of life.

**How the necessity layer is delivered.** Most of the credits and subsidies are paid automatically. Rent, utilities, health care, food, transit, and the other necessities the design covers are settled without the person applying, filing, or managing an account. For ordinary life, people do not have to see or operate the subsidy. Where a credit cannot meet the need, cash is there. The public books still show what was paid, at the federal, state, and community level, so the floor can be audited even when a household never touches it.

**What can be built now.** Employee ownership income exists under current law: employee stock ownership plans, employee ownership trusts, broad-based equity, and worker cooperatives. It is the layer that can be widened now, firm by firm, and then stacked onto the federal, state, and community funds and the automatic necessity floor as those are built. People inside a participating firm gain a claim on that firm. People without that job are carried by the other four layers.

**How place enters.** The federal layer is common. The state and community layers change with the place: local prices, local employers, and the local public balance sheet. A community with a thin tax base is not left to fund its own floor alone. The state and federal layers are what make the baseline hold in a poor place as well as a rich one. Tailoring changes the mix. It does not lower the floor.

**What the paper still has to show before this architecture can be recommended.**

- Who capitalizes each fund, in the year it is created, and who gives up that claim.
- How large the automatic necessity package is, in dollars, against a stated basket of necessities, and which items are credits, which are subsidies, and which are cash.
- That employee ownership is an addition to the floor for people who have the job, and that the other layers meet the floor for people who do not.
- How a household's income moves when profits, wages, and local asset prices fall in the same year. The automatic necessity layer has to keep paying in that year. A dividend that shrinks with the market does not, unless a backstop is specified and its cost is counted.
- How the books stay public, and how a person can see and correct a payment, while ordinary necessities stay automatic.

## This paper's scope

Settled by the author on 2026-10-20. The five-layer architecture is the puzzle. This paper is only the employee-ownership piece, and how that piece relates to the rest.

**Where the income comes from.** Employee ownership income is a capitalistic arrangement inside the firm. It is produced by the employee's work and by how that company is owned: a claim on equity, on surplus, or on a defined share of revenue or profit. It is not a check from the government. Federal, state, and community funds, and the universal basic income, are other layers. They are context here. They are not this paper's design problem.

**What government can do.** Policy can help businesses adopt these structures: tax treatment, succession rules, financing for worker buyouts, technical assistance, and rules that keep a plan broad-based. Those are enabling conditions. A tax incentive has a public cost, and the paper will say so. The income the employee receives still comes from the firm.

**What the paper discusses.**

1. **How ownership income is promoted.** The channels through which more employees come to hold a claim: owner succession, new plans at existing firms, cooperatives, trusts, and broad equity grants. Businesses are the actors. Policy is the help.
2. **The forms, compared on the same questions.** Employee stock ownership plans. Worker cooperatives. Employee ownership trusts. Broad-based equity: grants, options, and purchase plans. Profit sharing. Revenue sharing. For each: what the employee holds, whether the claim survives the job, who is eligible inside the firm, when cash can be spent, who bears the firm's risk, and what the evidence actually shows.
3. **Trade-offs.** A claim on the employing firm ties wealth to the same place as the wage, so a failure can hit both at once. A share of revenue or profit can pay cash sooner and still end when employment ends, with no residual stake. Cooperatives add a voice in governance and are harder to scale. Equity plans can build wealth and can be illiquid until retirement. Plans that cover only some workers, or only firms that choose them, leave everyone else out. The paper states these as the costs of a business-driven layer, and it says which form buys which benefit.
4. **What policymakers can do today.** Steps that use current law and current institutions in the United States. No new federal fund is required for this layer.
5. **The place in the AI future.** Employee ownership is the layer that can be widened before the other four exist. It gives people inside participating firms a claim on the earnings of those firms. It does not cover people without that job, and it does not guarantee the floor when the firm or the market falls. The other layers exist in the architecture to do that work. This paper's last job is to say that clearly, so employee ownership is not asked to be the whole system.

Profit sharing and revenue sharing are in the comparison because the author named them. The paper keeps one line straight: a payment that ends with the job and carries no claim on the firm is shared compensation. A claim that is ownership income is a residual stake in the company. Both can raise what the employee receives. They are not the same instrument.

## Audiences and the standard each one will apply

**Economics.** Definitions match national accounts or say why they depart. Incidence is named: who pays, who receives, what happens to investment. General-equilibrium effects are acknowledged, including a lower return on capital if the capital stock deepens. No causal claim without a design that could support it. Case studies are illustrations.

**Policy.** Each mechanism has an administrator, a legal hook, a transition path, a failure mode, and an interaction with taxes, pensions, disability insurance, and unemployment insurance. "Ownership" is not treated as self-executing.

**General public.** Every mechanism answers three plain questions: who gets a claim, what is the claim on, and when does cash arrive relative to a lost paycheck. A household that cannot wait out a lockup is not helped by a claim that pays in twenty years.

## Claim discipline

The author's overarching thesis, above, is the architecture under examination. The tests below are what each layer has to pass. Later threads may revise a test. They do not get to smuggle in a stronger claim without a section that earns it.

**Working thesis.** The five-layer floor mitigates wage disruption when three conditions hold at once:

1. The disruption is a lasting shift in factor shares, bargaining power, or the wage structure, not only a short spell of unemployment.
2. The household receives a claim on assets that earn the displaced income, and the claim is large relative to the lost wages.
3. The household can bear the delay, volatility, and illiquidity of capital returns, or a separate income floor covers those features.

If any condition fails, ownership income can raise lifetime wealth and still fail the person who lost the wage. UBI, or a narrower income floor, answers the third condition and does not answer the first two. Labor-demand policy (new tasks, worker-complementary technology, wage insurance while people move) answers a different question again: whether the wage channel itself can be rebuilt.

**Claims the paper is allowed to make, if the sections below are actually written:**

- A factor-share shift changes household income in proportion to how much of the household's income is wages versus claims on capital.
- Policies that broaden claims differ in who capitalizes them, who is included, how soon cash arrives, and what risk the household holds.
- At equal public cost, a transfer and an ownership grant buy different bundles: stability now versus a balance-sheet claim on future capital income.
- Some popular "ownership" policies are wage-saving plans or current taxes with a new label. The taxonomy must say so.

**Claims the paper will not make:**

- That AI will end employment, or that it will not.
- That ownership income replaces the welfare state, unemployment insurance, or schooling.
- That a dividend of a few hundred dollars a year offsets occupational wage loss.
- That forced broad ownership is free because "the robots pay for it." Capitalization is paid by someone: taxpayers, workers, existing owners, or future beneficiaries through lower returns.
- That UBI is unaffordable in principle, or that ownership is automatically affordable. Both have a resource cost. The paper compares the cost to what each one buys.
- A specific optimal tax rate, fund size, or dividend. Those require a model and data the outline does not yet contain.

## Research questions

1. Through which channels can AI and robotics reduce wage income while raising income to capital, and for which households is that a loss rather than a wash?
2. What, in accounting terms, is ownership income, and which household cash flows are easily mistaken for it?
3. By what mechanisms can policy assign capital claims more broadly, and what does each mechanism actually transfer?
4. Under what household circumstances does that assignment move someone to a higher, safer, or more resilient position, and under what circumstances does it miss them?
5. Holding fiscal or dilution cost fixed, how does broad ownership compare with UBI and with wage insurance, earnings subsidies, and retraining on income level, income risk, wealth, work incentives, investment incentives, inclusion, and political durability?
6. Which design rules stop a broadened claim from reconcentrating within a generation?

## Definitions the introduction must lock down

Use these consistently. If a later thread uses a word differently, the intake log should flag the conflict rather than blend the meanings.

**Wage income.** Labor earnings: wages, salaries, and self-employment income that is a return to the person's labor. Employer benefits tied to the job count as part of the compensation package the worker loses when the job goes.

**Wage disruption.** Any of: job loss, fewer hours, lower real wages in exposed tasks, weaker bargaining power, or a career that never forms. Disruption includes people who stay employed at worse terms. It is not a synonym for mass unemployment.

**Ownership income.** The return to a claim on productive assets: distributed profits, interest, rent, and the portion of self-employment income that is a return to the owner's capital. Accrued capital gains are changes in wealth, not current spendable income, until realized or borrowed against. The paper must keep that distinction or it will count paper gains as a paycheck.

**Ownership.** A claim with residual rights to an asset's returns. A tax-financed cash payment is a transfer even if advocates call it a dividend. A dividend is ownership income only if a corpus exists and the payment is a return on that corpus.

**Mitigation.** A smaller loss, or a gain, in the household outcomes listed in the welfare section. Mitigation is not "the economy is fine on average."

**Equal cost.** The same present-value resource cost, whether raised as tax, incurred as dilution of existing owners, or incurred as public borrowing. Comparisons that ignore capitalization are not comparisons.

**Predistribution.** Changing the claims people hold before market incomes are formed. Redistribution taxes those incomes and transfers cash. Some mechanisms sit on the line. The paper should place each mechanism rather than treat the words as praise.

## Paper structure

This is the spine for the employee-ownership paper. The five-layer floor stays in the thesis above. It is context in the introduction and in the closing section. It is not designed here.

### Abstract (150–200 words, written last)

The problem inside the firm. The distinction between a claim on the company and a share of current results. The main trade-offs. What businesses and policymakers can do now. Where the layer stops. No forecast of the end of work, and no ranking of ownership over wages that the evidence section has not earned.

**JEL codes to confirm:** J33, J54, G32, M52, O33.

**Keywords:** employee ownership; employee stock ownership plans; worker cooperatives; profit sharing; revenue sharing; artificial intelligence; business succession.

### 1. Introduction

Purpose: say what happens inside the firm when more of the surplus accrues to capital, and what this paper will and will not do.

- Separate exposure to AI from realized effects on jobs, hours, and pay.
- If more income goes to capital, it matters who holds the claim. Today that claim is concentrated.
- This paper is the firm. Employee ownership and income-sharing exist now. Businesses create the value. Policy can lower the cost of adopting a structure. Policy is not the source of the paycheck.
- The introduction carries the competing-interests statement: the author is Managing Partner at Forhemit, whose business includes structuring and financing transitions to 100% employee ownership; the paper is not investment advice and does not promote a firm, a transaction, or a product; mixed and unfavorable evidence is presented as fully as favorable evidence.
- Research questions: what the forms are; what the evidence shows for workers and firms; what the trade-offs are; what policymakers can do now; how the layer sits in a broader system it does not itself provide.
- The honest preview: a claim on the employing firm can share that firm's gains with the people who work there. It does not cover people outside the firm, and a balance paid at retirement is not this month's wage.

### 2. Definitions

Purpose: keep ownership, pay, and governance from being treated as the same thing.

Three rights, scored separately:

- **Ownership.** A direct or beneficial claim on the firm's equity or surplus.
- **Income-sharing.** A contract for part of profit, revenue, or a measured gain. It can end with the job and carry no residual stake.
- **Participation.** A voice in governance or in workplace decisions.

Three outcomes, reported separately:

- Spendable cash.
- Vested wealth that may not be spendable yet.
- What the household can fall back on if the job and the firm fail together.

A retirement plan that the tax code calls a profit-sharing plan does not have to be funded out of profits. A cash bonus calculated on profit is a different instrument. Phantom stock and stock appreciation rights are contracts for cash tied to value. They are ownership income only when the employee receives a share.

The paper's core variable is how closely the firm claim moves with the worker's own wage. The hypothesis is that a claim can raise what a worker holds in the firm. It is not an insurance instrument, because the return moves with that worker's own labor income. It can belong in a larger set of protections as an income-and-wealth layer. It cannot be the floor.

Six dimensions for every arrangement: whether there is a claim on assets; whether there is control; when cash can be spent; whether the risk is one firm or many; what happens on leaving, including displacement; and who funds the claim (existing owners, the employee, retained earnings, seller finance, debt, or a public incentive).

### 3. The shift inside the firm

Purpose: a short setup, not a second paper on automation.

- Task displacement, productivity, and new tasks. Automation can raise output per worker and still lower the labor share. New tasks can offset that. The paper does not pick one forecast.
- Inside a firm, when output grows and the claim on the residual stays with outside owners, the people doing the work do not hold that gain.
- Three scenarios, labeled as scenarios. The firm captures the AI gain as profit or enterprise value. Competition passes the gain through as a lower price. The firm is disrupted, so wages and the ownership claim fall together. Employee ownership captures a gain only when that employer captures it. If the value sits in firms with few employees, or if competition shows up as a lower price rather than a profit, this layer has little or nothing to distribute.

### 4. Forms

Purpose: the descriptive center. One template for each form: how it works, how common it is, who funds it, tax treatment, control, liquidity, risk, and what happens if the firm automates, shrinks, or fails.

**Claims on the firm**

- Employee stock ownership plans, including leveraged and unleveraged plans and the S-corporation form. Trust-held shares, allocations, valuation, repurchase obligations, and distributions that are often at departure or retirement.
- Employee ownership trusts. A lasting trust for employees, with benefits that depend on the trust document. Established in the United Kingdom. Not a fully specified U.S. statute.
- Worker cooperatives. Membership, one member one vote, patronage distributions. Small in the United States relative to employee stock ownership plans. Harder to finance at scale.
- Broad-based equity: restricted stock, options, and purchase plans. Common in some public and growth companies, often narrow in who is eligible.
- Direct purchase and hybrids.

**Shares of results, without a residual claim**

- Cash and deferred profit sharing, distinguished from a tax-qualified plan that only bears the name.
- Gainsharing on a measured improvement.
- Revenue sharing. Easier to audit than profit, and able to pay out when the firm is not actually earning.

**Contracts tied to value**

- Phantom equity and stock appreciation rights.
- Proposals that are not practice: equity for contractors, and claims that would survive the elimination of the job. Label them as proposals.

The matrix of forms by the six dimensions is the paper's main figure. Counts in that figure use the checked sources already in the intake log, with the year and the population on the number.

### 5. Trade-offs

Purpose: tensions, not a list of advantages.

| Tension | What the form can do | What it costs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Alignment and concentration | People share in the firm's success | Wages and wealth can fall with the same firm |
| Wealth and liquidity | Balances can compound | Many plans pay at exit, not during a job loss |
| Voice and speed | Cooperatives and some trusts add a say | Decision costs, and capital that is harder to raise |
| Addition and substitution | A claim on top of a wage | A plan that replaces pay with a riskier payment |
| Continuity and debt | A sale to employees can keep a firm intact | Leverage and repurchase obligations take cash |
| Simplicity and margins | Revenue shares are easy to see | Revenue can rise while the firm loses money |

Also: who inside the firm receives the claim (pay level, tenure, full-time status); who never gets a plan; and the equal-cost comparison — the same resources as higher wages, as a bonus, or as a claim — including a downturn. Report the median and the bad case, not only the average account.

Firms choose among these forms because of size, margins, whether the owner is selling, and whether the workforce is stable. That choice is the business-driven part of the paper.

### 6. Evidence

Purpose: what existing studies can support, and what they cannot.

Permitted, with the limits already written in the intake log:

- Scale: about 11 million active participants in employee stock ownership plans in 2023 filings, against a U.S. workforce on the order of 160 million. About 15 million participants if former employees still in the plans are counted. Total plan assets are not the same as employer stock.
- Worker cooperatives: the 2025 census cited by the Department of Labor, not a round number without a year.
- The 2017 comparison of workers ages 28 to 34: higher median household wealth, wages, and tenure, in dollar levels, and not a causal result.
- French worker cooperatives: smaller employment cuts in a sales drop, and more flexible pay. State both.
- Shared capitalism: associations with workplace outcomes are stronger when participation, training, and a real wage are also present.
- No substitution of company stock for pay in the Buchele, Kruse, Rodgers, and Scharf samples. That finding does not cover every form.
- Industry and center reports, including the National Center for Employee Ownership, are usable when labeled, and they do not carry causal claims by themselves.

The gap to name: almost no study tests these arrangements as insurance against an automation shock. Selection — better firms may be the ones that share ownership — stays in the text.

### 7. Why firms do this

Purpose: the capitalist case, as a set of motives to evaluate.

- An owner who is leaving can sell to the people who work there instead of to an outside buyer. State the opportunity without an uncounted "millions of firms" figure.
- Hiring, keeping people, and the quality of decisions, as a package with participation rather than as an effect of a grant alone.
- Tax results for the seller. Those are policy, and they have a public cost.
- Barriers: advisers, valuation, lawsuits and fiduciary duty, financing, and thin margins.

### 8. What policymakers can do now

Purpose: enabling rules. Separate law in force from bills.

In force, to be described from the statute when drafted: Section 1042 for certain sales to employee stock ownership plans and qualified worker cooperatives; S-corporation plan tax treatment; deductible contributions; Employee Retirement Income Security Act duties, including valuation; the Department of Labor's Employee Ownership Initiative; a $2 million appropriation in 2026 that is not yet evidence of grants paid; Small Business Administration 7(a) loans only within that program's actual rules.

A bill, not a program: S.2458, the Employee Ownership Financing Act, introduced 24 July 2025.

For each tax or financing idea, ask whether it creates an additional claim for employees or mainly pays the seller, the lender, or the adviser. Subsidized plans should be watched for replacing wages. Diversification rules should be described for the kind of plan they actually cover.

Rank levers by cost, how much new employee ownership they create, whether they can be done under current law, and how strong the evidence is.

### 9. Place in the larger system

Purpose: one section, not a second paper.

- Inside a participating firm, a claim can share that firm's gains, including gains from automation, with the people who still work there.
- It misses people without that job, people displaced before they vest, firms with nothing to distribute, and cases where the gain is a lower price or is captured by a supplier of the technology.
- A firm that automates away its own workforce also shrinks its set of owners.
- Federal, state, and community funds, and a cash floor, are the layers that would have to cover those gaps. This paper names the gaps. It does not design those layers.
- Design hooks, if the other layers are built later: a rollover into a diversified account, and a statement the person can read. Those are connections, not current programs.

### 10. Objections and conclusion

State the limits in the paper's own voice. About 11 million active participants is a small share of the workforce. Much of the value is wealth at exit, not income now. The evidence is selected. Tax breaks can pay for transactions that would have happened anyway. High-profile plan failures belong next to the successes.

Say what would weaken the account: evidence that plans systematically replace wages; evidence that the people who receive claims are only those who were already secure; evidence that, under the paper's own scenarios, the claim rarely becomes spendable when wages fall.

The conclusion restates the forms, the trade-offs, what can be done now, and the boundary. It does not predict how far AI will go, and it does not claim this layer is the floor.

Proposed research paper outline
Beyond Wages: Broadening Capital Ownership as a Resilience Strategy for AI- and Robotics-Driven Economic Change
Subtitle: Mechanisms, Evidence, and Complementary Public Policies for Distributing the Gains from Automation

Proposed paper type: Interdisciplinary economics and public-policy research paper combining a systematic evidence review, comparative institutional analysis, and distributional policy modeling.

Abstract
The paper will investigate whether, how, and under what conditions broader access to productive capital can reduce household vulnerability to AI- and robotics-driven changes in employment, wages, and labor demand. It will examine mechanisms that allow individuals to receive income or accumulate wealth from productive assets, including employee ownership, worker cooperatives, individual capital endowments, collective investment funds, public wealth funds, and citizen dividends.

These mechanisms will be compared with Universal Basic Income (UBI), social insurance, wage subsidies, retraining, and other policy responses. The analysis will assess distributional effects, economic incentives, fiscal sustainability, implementation barriers, and potential unintended consequences.

The paper will distinguish established empirical findings from theoretical arguments and model-based projections. Its objective is to identify a practical policy framework for broadening economic participation in the returns to technological progress without assuming that labor income will disappear or that ownership income can replace existing social protections.

1. Introduction: The Distributional Challenge of Technological Progress
1.1 The changing relationship between work and economic security
- The historical role of employment and wages in distributing the gains from economic production.
- How AI, industrial automation, and robotics may change labor demand, productivity, wages, and the organization of firms.
- The distinction between the elimination of jobs, transformation of tasks, reduced bargaining power, wage compression, and increased economic insecurity.
- Why changes in the distribution of productive assets may matter alongside changes in employment.
1.2 The central policy problem
- Productivity gains do not necessarily translate into broadly shared household income.
- The potential divergence between the people who contribute labor and the people who own the assets generating economic returns.
- Whether existing systems, heavily reliant on wages, taxation, and means-tested transfers, adequately address this challenge.
1.3 Research question
Under what economic and institutional conditions can broadening ownership of productive capital materially reduce household vulnerability to AI- and robotics-driven labor-market disruption, and how does ownership-based income compare with or complement UBI and other public policies?
1.4 Research objectives
1. Establish the economic mechanisms linking automation, labor income, capital ownership, and household financial security.
2. Identify and classify practical mechanisms for broadening access to ownership income.
3. Evaluate the empirical evidence for each mechanism and its limitations.
4. Compare ownership-based approaches with income transfers and other policy interventions.
5. Develop a framework for evaluating combined policy approaches under different technological and economic scenarios.
1.5 Contribution and scope
- Bring labor economics, capital ownership, wealth distribution, and social-protection policy into a common analytical framework.
- Evaluate actual mechanisms rather than treating ownership as a single policy.
- Distinguish policies that redistribute current income from policies that broaden ownership of productive assets.
- Use the United States as the primary case while drawing on relevant international examples.
2. Conceptual Framework and Definitions
2.1 Wage income, ownership income, and transfer income
Define three separate channels:
- Labor income: Wages, salaries, and other compensation earned through work.
- Ownership income: Dividends, interest, distributions, net rental income, royalties, cooperative patronage returns, and other cash flows attributable to ownership claims.
- Transfer income: Government or other qualifying transfers that are not contingent on employment or an individual's ownership of the underlying productive asset.
Capital gains should be reported separately from recurring cash income. Asset appreciation may increase wealth without providing spendable income.

Profit-sharing arrangements should also be distinguished from equity ownership when participation does not provide an actual ownership claim.
2.2 Ownership as wealth, income, and economic participation
- Wealth accumulation versus recurring income.
- Liquid versus illiquid assets.
- Individual versus collective ownership.
- Financial returns versus control, voting rights, and participation in decision-making.
- The distinction between owning an asset, receiving a share of its returns, and having access to a public dividend funded by collectively owned assets.
2.3 A proposed causal framework
The paper will investigate the following potential chain:

AI and robotics adoption → changes in productivity and labor demand → changes in the distribution of income between labor and capital → changes in household economic security.

Broadening access to productive assets may interrupt this chain by allowing more households to receive capital returns. The magnitude of the effect will depend on the value of the assets distributed, the returns they generate, participation rates, costs, risk, and the distribution of ownership.
2.4 Defining the intended outcomes
- Household income adequacy and stability.
- Ability to withstand unemployment or reduced wages.
- Wealth accumulation and distribution.
- Economic mobility across income groups and generations.
- Poverty, financial insecurity, and material well-being.
- Fiscal sustainability and macroeconomic resilience.
3. What Does the Evidence Say About AI, Labor Income, and Capital Returns?
3.1 What is known about AI and employment?
- Historical evidence from earlier automation waves.
- Evidence concerning generative AI, robotics, and complementary technologies.
- Job displacement versus task transformation, augmentation, and new job creation.
- Differences across occupations, industries, income groups, and countries.
3.2 Changes in labor income and the labor share
- Trends in labor's share of national income.
- Productivity growth compared with real wage growth.
- The roles of market concentration, globalization, capital intensity, and worker bargaining power.
- Whether observed trends establish a causal relationship with automation or reflect multiple interacting factors.
The 2025 ILO/OECD publication on labor-income inequality provides a relevant starting point for this analysis. The paper must not attribute the entire decline to AI.
3.3 The distribution of capital income and productive assets
- Existing disparities in the ownership of financial assets, businesses, and other productive property.
- How asset ownership relates to household income, inheritance, age, and socioeconomic position.
- Whether households exposed to wage disruption possess enough diversified assets to offset lost earnings.
3.4 Alternative interpretations and uncertainty
- Scenarios in which AI increases employment and wages through productivity and complementary work.
- Scenarios in which displacement, weaker bargaining power, or capital concentration intensifies inequality.
- The distinction between occupational exposure estimates, observed adoption, realized employment effects, and long-term projections.
Purpose of this section: Establish the problem empirically without assuming in advance that technological progress will cause mass unemployment.
4. Economic Mechanisms Through Which Ownership Income Could Improve Economic Security
4.1 Income diversification
Assess whether recurring returns from productive assets can reduce a household's dependence on wages as its sole significant income source.
4.2 Participation in productivity gains
Evaluate whether individuals who own claims on productive capital can benefit from economic growth even when their direct contribution of paid labor changes.
4.3 Wealth accumulation and compounding
Examine how recurring investments, reinvested returns, and capital endowments could build financial assets over time, including the effect of fees, debt, inflation, and unequal starting conditions.
4.4 Household resilience and consumption smoothing
Investigate whether diversified ownership income provides a buffer against unemployment, reduced hours, wage stagnation, or career transitions.
4.5 Bargaining power and economic autonomy
Examine whether alternative income sources change workers' ability to negotiate, retrain, reduce hours, start businesses, leave unsuitable employment, or undertake unpaid care.
4.6 Aggregate demand and macroeconomic stability
Assess whether wider ownership could distribute purchasing power more broadly and reduce economic instability associated with concentrated income and weak household demand.
4.7 Limits of the mechanisms
- Capital returns can be volatile or negative.
- Small ownership stakes may generate negligible income.
- Labor and capital income may decline together during downturns.
- Concentrated employer-stock holdings can expose workers to simultaneous job and investment losses.
- Ownership claims can be acquired through debt, leaving households more financially vulnerable rather than less.
The analysis will distinguish the plausible theoretical mechanism from evidence that a real-world program produces the intended effect.
5. Mechanisms for Broadening Ownership Income
Each mechanism will be evaluated using the same criteria: who receives ownership, how it is financed, how returns are generated, how income reaches households, who bears risk, and what evidence exists about outcomes.
5.1 Broad-based employee ownership
- Employee Stock Ownership Plans (ESOPs).
- Broad-based equity grants and stock purchase plans.
- Employee ownership trusts and employee buyouts.
- Ownership transitions for small and midsized businesses.

5.2 Worker cooperatives and democratic business ownership

- Shared governance and distribution of surplus.
- Cooperative conversion of existing businesses.
- Access to acquisition financing and professional management.
Distinguish the potential income benefits of ownership from the separate benefits or costs of democratic control.
5.3 Universal or broadly distributed capital endowments
- Capital grants or "baby bonds."
- Matched investment accounts.
- Publicly financed seed capital for young adults or other eligible populations.
- Rules governing investment, withdrawals, and permissible uses.
Assess whether a capital endowment creates a meaningful asset base, whether it improves access to homeownership, business creation, or financial assets, and whether restrictions enhance or undermine its usefulness.
5.4 Social wealth funds and public investment funds
- Publicly capitalized funds holding diversified productive assets.
- Universal or broadly targeted participation in fund returns.
- Citizen dividends or individual capital accounts linked to collective investment.
- Governance, investment mandates, distribution rules, and intergenerational equity.
Examine the distinction between distributing a fund's current earnings and liquidating assets to finance short-term payments.
5.5 Universal access to investment and retirement capital
- Automatic enrollment in diversified investment accounts.
- Public matching contributions for lower-income households.
- Tax-advantaged savings and retirement systems.
- Low-cost pooled investment and portable accounts.
Evaluate the extent to which these instruments reach people with insufficient disposable income to save voluntarily.
5.6 Community and shared productive asset ownership
- Community investment funds.
- Shared ownership of local businesses and productive infrastructure.
- Cooperative ownership of selected energy, computing, or other facilities.
- Mechanisms for distributing revenue within defined communities.
Assess local economic resilience, geographic inequality, governance capacity, and the risks of dependence on a narrow local asset base.
5.7 New and emerging ownership mechanisms
- Broad-based participation in private business and venture investment.
- Royalties, licensing income, and selected forms of intellectual-property ownership.
- Public or collective participation in returns from publicly supported research and infrastructure.
- Potential models for sharing returns from AI-related assets.
These mechanisms require careful legal and economic analysis. A right to access a service or a claim on data value is not necessarily equivalent to a viable, income-generating ownership stake.
5.8 Comparative assessment
For each mechanism, evaluate:
1. Potential recurring income per participant.
2. Number and type of people reached.
3. Time required to generate meaningful income.
4. Capital and administrative costs.
5. Risk, liquidity, and diversification.
6. Effects on inequality and social mobility.
7. Legal, governance, and implementation barriers.
8. Strength of empirical evidence.
6. Ownership Income Compared With UBI and Other Public Policies
6.1 Universal Basic Income
- Definition and alternative designs.
- Universal versus targeted eligibility.
- Transfer size, payment frequency, funding, and inflation-adjustment mechanisms.
- Effects on poverty, employment, financial security, administrative complexity, and individual autonomy.
6.2 Ownership income and UBI: Different economic functions
Ownership-based income provides a claim on the returns from assets. UBI provides a cash transfer independent of individual asset ownership or employment.

The paper should compare whether each approach provides sufficient income, how reliably payments arrive, who bears investment risk, and how the two approaches affect wealth distribution over time.
6.3 Additional policy alternatives
- Unemployment insurance and portable social insurance.
- Wage insurance and earned-income subsidies.
- Retraining, education, and employment transition support.
- Job guarantees and public employment programs.
- Shorter working hours and work-sharing.
- Universal basic services, including relevant health, education, housing, and transportation policies.
- Minimum wages, collective bargaining, and worker protections.
6.4 Complementarity and substitution
Examine which interventions should be evaluated as alternatives and which perform different functions.

For example, transfers can provide immediate and predictable income support, while ownership accumulation may take years to produce meaningful returns. Conversely, ownership may offer access to wealth growth that a fixed transfer alone does not provide.
6.5 Evidence from existing programs
Use carefully selected cases, potentially including the Alaska Permanent Fund Dividend, employee ownership programs in the United States, publicly owned investment funds, and capital-endowment initiatives.

The Alaska dividend is particularly useful as a comparison, although its results cannot simply be generalized to a national, fully funded UBI. Published research found no significant reduction in aggregate employment and a modest increase in part-time work.

7.1 Potential sources of capital
- Voluntary household and employer contributions.
- Tax incentives and matching contributions.
- Public capitalization and investment of public revenues.
- Inheritance and wealth-transfer policy.
- Returns from existing public assets.
- Carefully designed contributions related to resource rents, intellectual property, or other identifiable revenue sources.
7.2 Financing ongoing distributions
Distinguish funds used to purchase assets from the recurring revenue needed to sustain cash payments, administration, and risk reserves.
7.3 Effects on investment and innovation
- Potential effects on capital formation and entrepreneurship.
- Corporate financing and control.
- Employer responses to ownership incentives.
- Tax-design effects on investment, productivity, and the location of economic activity.
7.4 Distributional and generational trade-offs
- Who pays and who benefits?
- Does the policy expand net ownership or merely transfer existing assets?
- How should returns be balanced between current recipients and future generations?
- How can benefits reach people with little initial wealth?
7.5 Fiscal stress testing
Model the consequences of weaker investment returns, recessions, slower growth, rising participation, and higher administrative costs.

8.1 Literature review
Conduct a transparent, reproducible review of academic literature, government data, institutional research, and relevant policy evaluations.

Document search terms, inclusion criteria, exclusion criteria, geographic scope, publication dates, and the treatment of conflicting evidence.
8.2 Evidence hierarchy
Classify findings as:
- Causal empirical evidence.
- Strong observational evidence.
- Descriptive evidence.
- Theoretical results.
- Model-based projections.
- Policy proposals with limited outcome data.
Do not treat all forms of evidence as equally conclusive.
8.3 Comparative case studies
Select programs that differ in ownership structure, financing, distribution rules, target population, and governance. Document the context in which each program operates and whether its findings can reasonably transfer to another setting.
8.4 Distributional and fiscal modeling
Where data permit, construct household-level or representative-household scenarios that compare:
- A baseline without additional intervention.
- Ownership expansion alone.
- UBI or another transfer alone.
- Combined ownership and income-support policies.
Estimate the effects on disposable income, wealth distribution, income volatility, poverty, participation, and fiscal requirements.
8.5 Core measurement framework
Potential primary indicators include:
- Net recurring ownership income as a share of household income.
- The proportion of households receiving positive ownership income.
- The proportion receiving enough ownership income to offset a defined wage-income reduction.
- Changes in disposable-income inequality and wealth concentration.
- Household resilience under specified employment and income shocks.
- Public cost per participant and cost per unit of measured improvement.
Distinguish gross asset values, unrealized capital gains, and cash actually available to households.
8.6 Scenario and sensitivity analysis
Test multiple assumptions about technology adoption, wage effects, capital returns, asset participation, program scale, taxation, and economic cycles.

Report where the results change materially when assumptions change.
8.7 Research limitations
Discuss selection bias, reverse causality, limited long-term evidence, incomparable program designs, model uncertainty, and the difficulty of projecting future AI-related economic change.
9. Testable Hypotheses
The paper will test, rather than presume, the following propositions:

H1. Income diversification: Households with meaningful, diversified ownership income experience smaller declines in disposable income following labor-income shocks than otherwise comparable households.

H2. Distribution matters: Expanding access to productive assets reduces wealth and income inequality only when participation is sufficiently broad and net benefits reach households that otherwise own few productive assets.

H3. Institutional design matters: Ownership arrangements with appropriate diversification, governance, and risk protections perform better on household resilience than arrangements that concentrate household wealth in a single employer or asset.

H4. Scale and timing matter: The capacity of ownership-based policies to offset labor-income disruption depends on the size of the asset base, net returns, payout rules, and the time required to accumulate meaningful wealth.

H5. Complementarity: Combining broad ownership with a dependable income-support floor provides better protection across a wider range of economic scenarios than relying exclusively on either mechanism, though the magnitude and fiscal value of this benefit remain empirical questions.

H6. Unmitigated concentration: Where technological returns are concentrated among existing capital owners, and no sufficiently broad offsetting mechanism develops, AI adoption can increase wealth inequality even if some forms of wage inequality decline.

- Which mechanisms create recurring income, and which primarily build long-term wealth?
- What scale is necessary for ownership income to become economically material?

- Under what circumstances do UBI and other policies provide superior protection?

11.2 A potential layered policy architecture
Evaluate, without presupposing its superiority, a combination of:
1. A dependable income and essential-services floor.
2. Broad-based access to productive ownership.
3. Additional protections for people experiencing severe or persistent labor-market disruption.
4. Governance and disclosure rules to ensure beneficiaries receive the intended economic returns.
11.3 Implementation pathways

- Medium-term pilots that test ownership and funding mechanisms.

11.4 Evaluation and accountability

Summarize what the evidence establishes about ownership income as a possible mitigation to technological labor-market disruption. Identify the conditions under which it may improve income resilience, the circumstances in which it is insufficient, and the complementary role of other policies.

# Working Title

**Owning the Machine: Ownership Income as a Structural Mitigation for AI-Induced Wage Disruption**

Alternative: *Beyond the Check: Ownership Income, Capital Broadening, and the Policy Architecture of AI Adjustment*

# Abstract (working argument)

Generative AI and robotics are beginning to produce measurable displacement and wage-depressing effects in exposed tasks, while the returns to AI accrue disproportionately to the owners of compute, models, data infrastructure, and intangible capital. Conventional wage-replacement policies—most prominently universal basic income (UBI)—transfer a *flow* but leave the underlying *stock* of productive assets concentrated. This paper argues that **ownership income**—income derived from broadly held claims on capital—can function as a structural mitigation because it gives households a claim on the capital rents that AI intensifies, rather than relying solely on redistribution of labor income or deficit-financed transfers. The paper develops a mechanism-based framework, reviews the empirical evidence on ownership-broadening institutions, and compares ownership income with UBI on efficiency, distributional, political-economy, and macroeconomic grounds. It concludes that ownership income and UBI are neither substitutes nor ideological opposites: the strongest policy architecture is likely a **capital-broadening complement to, not a replacement for, a minimum income floor**—but only if ownership vehicles are designed to avoid concentration, preserve risk diversification, and reach populations that existing asset-holding institutions exclude.

# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 The policy question
- AI and robotics are not simply automating tasks; they are changing the **relative return to labor versus capital** and the **ownership structure of productive assets**.
- The policy debate has largely bifurcated between **wage-floor / transfer approaches** (UBI, wage subsidies, public employment) and **tax-and-redistribute approaches** (capital taxation, robot taxes). Ownership income is a distinct third channel: **broadening the asset base itself**.
- Core question: *Under what conditions, and through which institutional mechanisms, can ownership income materially offset AI-induced wage disruption without reproducing the concentration it is meant to remedy?*

## 1.2 Why this matters now
- Evidence of AI-related labor-market adjustment is accumulating: task-level substitution and wage depression in exposed work; relative employment declines for early-career workers in highly AI-exposed occupations; and rising worker anxiety about job loss.

# 2. Conceptual Framework: From Wage Income to Ownership Income

## 2.1 Definitions and boundaries
- **Wage income**: compensation for labor services, including benefits and variable pay tied to employment.
- **Ownership income**: income accruing from claims on productive assets—dividends, capital gains, interest, rents, profits, and distributed returns from collectively held funds. Distinguish **direct ownership** (household equity, employee stock) from **intermediated ownership** (sovereign wealth funds, citizen accounts, public equity pools).
- **UBI**: a periodic unconditional cash transfer financed from general revenues or dedicated taxes; a *flow* transfer, not a stock claim.
- **Near-cash transfers and wage subsidies**: relevant comparison but analytically distinct.

## 2.2 Why the functional distribution matters
- The standard labor-displacement frame focuses on employment counts. The ownership-income frame focuses on **factor shares and the asset distribution**.
- If AI raises capital’s share and if capital ownership is concentrated, then even full employment at stagnant real wages can produce rising inequality. The relevant inequality is not only between employed and unemployed but between **owners and non-owners of AI-augmenting capital**.
- Piketty’s \(r > g\) mechanism is a useful entry point: when returns to capital exceed growth, wealth concentrates structurally. AI may intensify this if the marginal product of compute and data infrastructure rises faster than the marginal product of ordinary labor.

## 2.3 Three transmission channels from ownership to household welfare
1. **Income channel**: ownership income adds a second income stream, reducing dependence on a single labor-market attachment.
2. **Claim channel**: ownership gives households a legal claim on the surplus generated by AI-intensive production, so productivity gains need not be fully mediated by wage bargaining.
3. **Bargaining and political-economy channel**: broad asset ownership changes preferences over taxation, regulation, and corporate governance, potentially sustaining pro-growth and pro-distribution coalitions.

## 2.4 What ownership income is *not*
- It is not a guaranteed positive return; capital income is volatile and procyclical.
- It is not automatically egalitarian: narrowly held employee stock or undiversified citizen accounts can concentrate risk.
- It is not a substitute for a minimum income floor in the presence of liquidity constraints and adverse shocks.

# 3. The Disruption: Evidence and Projections

## 3.1 Task-level and occupation-level evidence
- Task-level studies find a **modest but statistically significant wage premium in augmented tasks** and a **wage depression in pure substitution tasks**, with substantial heterogeneity across occupations and skill levels.
- Distributional estimates from the Minneapolis Fed suggest **moderate AI exposure benefits workers on average, but high exposure harms them**, with large within-occupation dispersion; returns to social skills rise while returns to analytical skills fall, and low-earners may gain more than high-earners in some configurations. This heterogeneity is critical: ownership income must be evaluated against a **distribution of outcomes**, not a single average effect.
- Early-career employment effects appear in some high-exposure occupations, while aggregate wage effects may lag employment effects.

## 3.2 The capital-share channel
- AI may intensify markups, rents, and the importance of intangible and infrastructure capital, contributing to a declining labor share in advanced economies.
- The “rentier asset” view argues that AI infrastructure concentration shifts returns toward those who **control access** to models, compute, and data, rather than toward the workers who apply AI in production. This is the strongest theoretical case for ownership-broadening: if the return is a rent on access, then broad ownership of the rent-generating asset is the direct distributional remedy.

## 3.3 Fiscal and macroeconomic projections
- NBER scenario analysis indicates that AI-driven growth can reduce federal debt substantially, but the fiscal benefit is smaller when income accrues to capital alone because capital income faces lower effective tax rates; when gains accrue to both capital and labor, the fiscal improvement is much larger.
- This is a key point for the ownership-income case: **the tax base erodes when labor’s share falls**, so an ownership-broadening strategy can also be a **tax-base-preserving strategy** if it converts some capital income into broadly held, taxable household income.

## 3.4 Non-income harms
- Job loss is not only an income shock; survey evidence suggests work provides purpose, social contact, and dignity, and a majority of respondents view a jobless AI future as “mostly a bad thing”.
- Ownership income does not by itself solve the **meaning and social-organization** problem. The paper should be explicit that it addresses the **income and claim** dimension, not the entire human cost of displacement.

# 4. Mechanisms of Ownership Income: A Taxonomy

This section is the analytical core. Each mechanism should be evaluated on five criteria: **(i) coverage, (ii) progressivity, (iii) risk diversification, (iv) fiscal cost, and (v) political durability**.

## 4.1 Broad-based employee ownership and profit sharing
- **Instruments**: ESOPs, broad-based stock option plans, employee ownership trusts, profit-sharing, cooperative conversion.
- **Evidence**: The large-sample literature is not uniformly positive, but the weight of evidence suggests employee ownership is linked to better outcomes on average, with positive and neutral findings; effects depend on plan breadth, firm size, and complementary high-performance workplace practices. Broad-based plans have been associated with productivity levels 20–33% higher than comparable firms in some periods, while small ESOPs show positive productivity effects and large ESOPs are more neutral.
- **AI-specific simulation**: In a high-adoption scenario, expanded employee ownership generates additional capital income to workers on the order of 5 percentage points of value added, largely offsetting the decline in workers’ combined wage-plus-capital claim.
- **Limitations**: employee ownership concentrates **human-capital risk and financial risk in the same firm**; it does not reach the unemployed, informal workers, or workers in small firms without tradable equity; and valuation and liquidity are problematic.

## 4.2 Sovereign wealth funds and citizen dividends
- **Instruments**: resource-based permanent funds (Alaska model), AI/compute permanent funds, public equity funds with per-capita dividends.
- **Evidence**: The Alaska Permanent Fund Dividend has **no measurable effect on aggregate employment**, increases part-time work by about 1.8 percentage points (17%), and is associated with substantial reductions in poverty—20–40% in some estimates—including among rural Indigenous populations and seniors.
- **Scalability to AI**: Proposals include requiring AI firms to transfer a portion of corporate value to a sovereign fund that distributes dividends to the public; the Alaska model demonstrates administrative feasibility and political durability over four decades.
- **Limitations**: resource rents are not the same as competitive AI rents; fund returns are volatile; dividend levels may be small relative to wage losses unless the fund is capitalized at a very large scale; and sovereign funds can raise governance and political-investment concerns.

## 4.3 Universal basic capital (UBC) / citizen equity accounts
- **Definition**: every resident receives a **stock** of equity—typically in a diversified portfolio or a citizens’ wealth fund—rather than only a periodic flow. UBC is explicitly framed as a “new twist” on UBI: instead of mailing checks from future tax revenue, it gives people a compounding ownership stake that is less vulnerable to shifting political winds once granted.
- **Theoretical case**: Growiec, Prettner, and Szkróbka argue that policies broadly distributing rents from capital—including compute and robots—such as UBC or capital-tax-financed UBI are **most likely to achieve lasting inequality reduction** under transformative AI.
- **Design spectrum**: (a) one-time or phased endowment of diversified equity; (b) restricted accounts with lock-ups and portfolio regulation; (c) “baby bonds” or matched savings; (d) hybrid UBC with a small periodic dividend.
- **Limitations**: transition financing; intergenerational equity; risk of account erosion through consumption; portfolio political interference; and the fact that a one-time grant may be insufficient for households facing persistent wage stagnation rather than a single shock.

## 4.4 Data dividends and public claims on AI value creation
- **Instruments**: data royalties, AI value-creation levies, public equity stakes in AI firms, revenue-sharing agreements for training data, and citizen dividends from public AI infrastructure.
- **Proposals**: A five-pillar model combining an AI value-creation levy, public data royalties, a direct citizen dividend, public AI infrastructure, and coexistence guarantees for individual copyrights. The Global AI Governance Initiative launched in October 2026 establishes transparency and revenue-sharing frameworks for data-contributing regions, though enforcement remains the critical test.
- **Normative foundation**: LLMs are built on collectively accumulated human knowledge; if that epistemic commons is privatized while knowledge workers are displaced, there is a distributive-justice claim to participation in AI value creation.
- **Limitations**: measurement of data contribution is contested; royalties may be passed through to consumers; international coordination is difficult; and the revenue base may be smaller than advocates expect.

## 4.5 Individual capital-income enhancement through savings and financial inclusion
- **Instruments**: automatic enrollment in diversified savings accounts, matched savings, child development accounts, portable benefit accounts, and targeted financial instruments for workers with limited access to welfare.
- **Evidence**: The Harvard Kennedy School working paper argues that enhancing individual capital income through increased household savings and targeted financial tools can help workers manage AI transitions, reduce fiscal pressures, and promote more equitable distribution of AI-driven capital gains, especially in countries with low household savings and limited social protection.
- **Limitations**: savings-based approaches are regressive in the short run because they require contributory capacity; they do not reach the liquidity-constrained; and they may be too slow to offset rapid displacement.

## 4.6 Public and collective ownership variants
- **Instruments**: public equity stakes, state-owned investment funds, cooperative ownership, community wealth funds.
- **Theoretical advantage**: captures rents at the source and can be structured to distribute per-capita.
- **Political-economy disadvantage**: public ownership raises governance, crowding-out, and political allocation concerns; the NBER discussion of policy options includes public ownership as one of four approaches, alongside UBI, higher capital taxes, and other transfers.

# 5. Why Ownership Income Can Move Individuals to a Better Place

## 5.1 Income diversification and resilience
- A second income stream reduces vulnerability to a single employer, sector, or task bundle. This is the **portfolio logic** of household income.
- Diversification is especially valuable when AI exposure is occupation-specific and difficult to insure.

## 5.2 A claim on the AI surplus
- Ownership income links household welfare to the **growth of the capital stock**, not only to the wage bill.
- If AI raises the capital share, broad ownership allows households to benefit from that shift instead of relying entirely on redistribution through the tax system.

## 5.3 Asset-building and long-run wealth accumulation
- Ownership income can compound; a flow transfer does not. This is the strongest argument for **stock-based** mechanisms over pure flow mechanisms in a growth economy.
- However, compounding benefits accrue disproportionately to those who can hold assets without liquidation, so design must prevent early erosion.

## 5.4 Bargaining and political economy
- Broad asset ownership may create a constituency for **pro-market, pro-productivity policies** because owners share in the upside.
- It may also reduce the political appeal of purely redistributive or protectionist responses to automation.

## 5.5 What ownership income does *not* solve
- It does not guarantee a **liquid minimum** for households with no savings and no wage.
- It does not address **care work, community contribution, and the social value of work**.
- It does not solve the **transition timing** problem: asset accumulation is slow relative to a displacement shock.

# 6. Ownership Income versus UBI: A Structured Comparison

This section should avoid a binary “ownership good, UBI bad” framing. The paper’s credibility depends on a **conditional and multidimensional comparison**.

## 6.1 Criteria for comparison
1. **Adequacy**: can the instrument replace a meaningful share of lost wage income?
2. **Incidence**: who receives the benefit, and how does it vary with income, wealth, age, and employment status?
3. **Labor-supply effects**: does it discourage work, and for whom?
4. **Fiscal cost and financing**: what is the required revenue, and what is the deadweight cost?
5. **Risk and volatility**: is the income stable across the business cycle?
6. **Political durability**: can it survive a change in government?
7. **Macroeconomic effects**: demand, inflation, investment, and the tax base.
8. **Transition speed**: how quickly can it reach displaced workers?

## 6.2 The UBI evidence base
- The UBI literature is **mixed and contested**. A synthesis of pilot evidence finds promise in poverty alleviation, economic security, and well-being, but limited efficacy as a singular panacea, with concerns about fiscal feasibility, inflation, and non-monetary aspects of work.
- A review of 122 guaranteed-income pilots finds that among 30 randomized pilots with published employment results, the average employment effect was **+0.8 percentage points**, but among the four largest treatment groups—accounting for 55% of treatment participants—the mean effect was **−3.2 percentage points**, with an estimated income elasticity of −0.18.
- Alaska’s permanent dividend is a useful natural experiment: it is universal and permanent, and it shows **no aggregate employment decline**, but it is a relatively small dividend and is financed by resource rents, not by broad capital taxation.
- The honest conclusion is not “UBI fails” or “UBI works”; it is that **labor-supply effects are heterogeneous, larger in high-intensity and longer-duration transfers, and likely concentrated among secondary earners, caregivers, and part-time workers**.

## 6.3 The ownership-income evidence base
- Employee ownership: positive average effects but substantial heterogeneity; breadth and complementary practices matter.
- Alaska: administratively feasible, politically durable, poverty-reducing, and employment-neutral in aggregate.
- UBC and capital-tax-financed UBI: strong theoretical case under transformative AI, but limited empirical implementation at scale.
- Data dividends: emerging governance frameworks but weak evidence on magnitude and incidence.

## 6.4 Comparative assessment
- **UBI advantages**: immediate, liquid, universal, administratively simple, reaches those with no asset base, and provides a floor.
- **UBI disadvantages**: a pure flow transfer does not change ownership concentration; it may be fiscally large and politically fragile; and it may have larger labor-supply effects at high benefit levels.
- **Ownership-income advantages**: addresses the stock of capital, can compound, aligns household income with productivity growth, and may be more politically durable once assets are granted.
- **Ownership-income disadvantages**: volatile, slow to build, often tied to employment or residency, regressive in transition if tax-financed, and vulnerable to concentration and governance failures.
- **Complementarity hypothesis**: ownership income is strongest as a **capital-broadening layer** on top of a **modest universal floor**; UBI is strongest as a **liquidity and adequacy layer** that ownership accounts cannot provide in the short run.

# 7. Policy Design and Implementation Architecture

## 7.1 Financing
- **Capital taxation**: progressive corporate income tax, capital gains taxation, inheritance taxation, and AI-specific levies. A progressive corporate income tax has been proposed as a mechanism to respond to erosion of the labor-tax base from AI.
- **AI value-creation levy**: a levy on AI revenues or compute, earmarked for a citizens’ fund.
- **Data royalties**: public data royalties and revenue-sharing agreements.
- **Asset transfers**: requiring AI firms to transfer equity to a public fund, as in some sovereign-fund proposals.
- **Avoid double-counting**: the paper must model the **combined fiscal cost** of a floor plus ownership accounts, not treat them as costless alternatives.

## 7.2 Institutional vehicles
- **Citizens’ wealth fund** with statutory dividend rules and independent governance.
- **Portable ownership accounts** with diversified default portfolios and restrictions on early withdrawal.
- **Employee ownership trusts** with broad-based eligibility and governance rights.
- **Public data trusts** that negotiate royalties on behalf of contributors.

## 7.3 Targeting and universalism
- Universal accounts may be politically attractive but expensive; targeted accounts (e.g., child accounts, low-wealth households) may be more progressive per dollar.
- The paper should distinguish **universal eligibility** from **progressive capitalization**: everyone can have an account, but public contributions can be larger for those with lower lifetime earnings and wealth.

## 7.4 Risk management
- **Diversification**: prohibit default concentration in employer stock; use broad index portfolios.
- **Liquidity**: allow limited withdrawals for emergencies, education, and housing, but preserve long-run compounding.
- **Insurance**: combine ownership accounts with unemployment insurance and a minimum income floor.
- **Governance**: independent investment boards, transparency, and conflict-of-interest rules.

## 7.5 Transition sequencing
- **Short run (0–3 years)**: expand unemployment insurance, wage insurance, and a temporary liquidity floor; pilot ownership accounts for displaced workers.
- **Medium run (3–10 years)**: capitalize a citizens’ fund from AI levies and data royalties; scale employee ownership via tax incentives and technical assistance.
- **Long run (10+ years)**: universal ownership accounts with progressive public contributions; integrate with the tax system to preserve the capital-income tax base.

## 7.6 Implementation challenges
- Valuation of AI firms and data contributions.
- International tax competition and capital mobility.
- Administrative capacity for account management and fund governance.
- Political resistance from incumbent asset owners.
- Legal constraints on mandatory equity transfers.

# 8. Distributional, Ethical, and Political-Economy Analysis

## 8.1 Who gains and who loses
- **Winners**: low- and middle-wealth households with long time horizons; workers in firms with broad-based ownership; residents of jurisdictions with strong funds.
- **Losers**: concentrated capital owners facing dilution or taxation; workers with short time horizons and high liquidity needs if ownership accounts are restricted; employees of firms that fail while holding concentrated stock.
- **Transitional losers**: older workers close to retirement who cannot wait for accounts to compound.

## 8.2 Intergenerational justice
- Capital-broadening financed by debt shifts burdens to future generations; financed by current capital taxation shifts burdens to current asset owners.
- A citizens’ fund can be structured as a **perpetual trust** that preserves principal for future generations while distributing a sustainable return.

## 8.3 The work and meaning objection
- Ownership income addresses the **income** dimension of displacement but not the **purpose, identity, and social contribution** dimension. The paper should acknowledge this explicitly and avoid claiming that ownership income is a complete answer to technological unemployment.
- Survey evidence shows that a majority of respondents view a jobless future as “mostly a bad thing,” and that work provides psychosocial benefits beyond cash. Ownership income should therefore be paired with **active labor-market policy, care infrastructure, and civic contribution institutions**.

## 8.4 Political durability
- Once granted, equity stakes create **property rights** that are harder to retract than annual transfer programs. This is a genuine advantage of stock-based ownership over flow-based UBI.
- But property rights also create **lock-in** and may constrain future redistribution if the asset distribution itself becomes politically entrenched.

## 8.5 Feasibility and institutional trust
- Sovereign funds and public equity stakes require high state capacity and low corruption; otherwise, they may become vehicles for patronage.
- Employee ownership requires functioning capital markets, valuation infrastructure, and legal frameworks for trusts and cooperatives.

# 9. Empirical Strategy and Evidence Gaps

- AI exposure measures are noisy and endogenous to firm and worker sorting.
- Employee ownership is not randomly assigned; positive effects may reflect selection.

It is to examine how society might organize ownership, public wealth, and essential services so that every citizen has a dependable economic foundation, while more people participate directly in the wealth created by technological progress.

## 1. The overarching thesis

I would express the proposed thesis this way:

## A Distributed Ownership and Economic Security Framework

Technological progress should expand the capacity for human flourishing, not leave economic security excessively dependent on continued access to paid employment.

A resilient economic system could combine broad-based employee ownership, public investment funds at multiple levels of government, community wealth-building institutions, and a guaranteed floor of essential economic support. Together, these mechanisms could give people both a greater stake in productive wealth and reliable access to the resources needed for a dignified life.

The framework would combine recurring ownership income with cash payments, credits, subsidies, and publicly financed services. Benefits could be coordinated and delivered automatically wherever feasible, with entitlements adapted to individual circumstances, local costs, and community needs.

The research would examine the conditions under which this framework is economically viable, institutionally feasible, equitable, and resilient to AI-driven changes in employment and income.

The thesis has two connected ambitions: broaden who owns the productive economy, and ensure that economic security does not depend entirely on owning assets or earning wages.

Those ambitions reinforce each other, but they are not interchangeable. A person may have access to essential support without owning productive assets, and a person may own assets without receiving enough income to meet basic needs.

## 2. The architecture: four interconnected components

1. Public and community wealth

Build shared productive capital

Federal, state, and community-level investment funds hold diversified assets and invest for the long term. Their investment returns could support public services, economic-security programs, community investment, or citizen dividends, according to their mandates and funding rules.

The important distinction is between accumulating assets for future generations and distributing a sustainable share of returns to people today.

2. Broad-based employee ownership

Give people a direct stake in productive enterprises

Employee-owned businesses, ESOPs, worker cooperatives, and other appropriately designed ownership arrangements can give workers claims on company value and earnings.

This is the immediately actionable component. In the United States, the National Center for Employee Ownership reported more than $2 trillion in assets held by identified ESOP plans, based on the latest available plan data in its January 2026 update. That demonstrates an established institutional base, not proof that ownership benefits are distributed adequately or that they replace wage income.

3. A dependable economic floor

Guarantee access to essential resources

A combination of cash, credits, subsidies, insurance, and directly provided services would help ensure access to an agreed minimum standard of living. The mix could reflect differences in housing costs, healthcare systems, transportation, disability-related needs, family size, and available local services.

UBI would be one possible component, not necessarily the entire system. Some needs are better served by flexible cash; others may be more reliably or efficiently addressed through insurance, direct services, or targeted subsidies. Their relative effectiveness is a research question.

4. An integrated delivery system

Make support seamless, accessible, and accountable

Benefits could be coordinated across programs and automatically applied to eligible expenses where appropriate. For example, a housing subsidy might go directly toward rent, healthcare coverage might be handled through an insurance system, and a cash benefit might be deposited into a person's account.

The intended experience is simple: people receive what they qualify for without navigating a maze of applications. The underlying system must still provide clear statements, privacy protections, correction procedures, and meaningful control over benefits.

Productive economy

Ownership channels

Employee equity, cooperatives, public and community funds

Public revenue

Taxes, existing revenues, and other legally available funding sources

Economic security framework

Ownership income + a dependable floor of cash, credits, subsidies, insurance, and essential services

## 3. The distinction that makes this research different

I would organize the research around three layers of economic security.

| Layer | Fundamental question | Intended function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Economic floor | What should everyone be able to count on? | Ensure access to essential resources, regardless of employment or asset ownership. |
| Ownership participation | Who receives the returns generated by the economy? | Broaden access to productive wealth and recurring capital income. |
| Economic opportunity | How can people improve their circumstances beyond the floor? | Support work, entrepreneurship, savings, education, business ownership, and individual choice. |

These layers matter because the research should not require a person to become an employee-owner, an investor, or an entrepreneur before being entitled to basic economic security.

Likewise, the ownership component should do more than prevent poverty. Its purpose is to investigate whether people can participate in the accumulation of wealth generated by the economy, rather than remaining solely recipients of wages or transfers.

## 4. How the paper should treat the role of employee ownership

Employee ownership is the starting mechanism, not the end-state architecture.

The paper should investigate three stages:

1. Stage 1: Expand ownership using existing institutions.

   Examine employee ownership transitions, ESOPs, cooperatives, and broad-based equity arrangements. Measure wealth accumulation, recurring distributions, access across wage levels, financial risk, and business sustainability.
2. Stage 2: Extend ownership beyond individual employers.

- **State/community layers**: capture **immobile local rents**—land value uplift around data centers, energy, water, local natural resources, local data—which a federal fund cannot easily claim. This is the Tiebout/Oates logic applied to capital.
- **Community layer**: addresses the **take-up and trust problem**—local institutions reach people that national programs miss, and local ownership creates local political constituencies.

- **Zero friction for the recipient** (automatic enrollment, automatic eligibility, no application, no recertification).
