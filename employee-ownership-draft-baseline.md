# Draft baseline: employee ownership income

**Status:** Baseline for drafting. Not a finished paper. Claims below are the argument the outline is allowed to make. Figures are only those already checked in the intake log, and they stay provisional until the definition, the date, and the primary source are confirmed in the draft. Nothing here is a new empirical result.

**Author:** Stefano Stokes, Managing Partner, Forhemit.

**Disclosure / competing interests.** The author is Managing Partner at Forhemit, a private equity firm whose business includes structuring and financing transitions to 100% employee ownership. The author therefore has a commercial interest in the employee ownership sector. This paper is not intended to promote any particular firm, transaction, or financial product, and no claim in it should be read as investment advice. The author has sought to present evidence on employee ownership—including evidence that is mixed, conditional, or unfavorable—as fully as the evidence on favorable outcomes, and he invites scrutiny of both the analysis and the conclusions.

## The argument in brief

Employee ownership and income-sharing are ways a firm can give the people who work there a claim on that firm’s results. The claim is created by how the company is owned and how pay is designed. The money comes from the enterprise: work, capital, technology, management, and the market together. It is not a measure of one person’s output, and it is not a check from the government. A claim can raise what a worker holds in the firm that employs them. It is not insurance against that firm doing badly, because the return moves with the worker’s own wage. It can sit beside other protections. It cannot be the floor.

Public policy in the United States has shaped whether firms adopt these structures. Sellers can defer gain on certain sales to an employee stock ownership plan or a qualified worker cooperative. Contributions can be deductible. S-corporation plans have a favorable tax treatment. Those rules have a public cost. They are not the source of the employee’s return. The accurate description is business-driven and policy-enabled.

As artificial intelligence and robotics change how firms produce, more of the surplus may accrue to capital. A worker with a claim on the employing firm can share in a gain that firm actually captures. The worker does not share a gain that never appears as profit, a gain already capitalized into the price paid for the shares, or a gain captured by a supplier of the technology. For a leveraged employee stock ownership plan that buys the company at fair market value, the employees are paying for the gains the valuation already included. Later gains have to exceed that price, and debt service can consume them. A claim on one firm is not a hedge against that firm’s failure: the job and the stake can fall together. It shares a broader automation gain only if that firm captures the gain. A worker who never holds the job, or who leaves before vesting, receives nothing from this layer.

This paper compares the forms, states the trade-offs, and says what businesses and policymakers can do now. It does not design a federal fund, a state fund, a community fund, or a universal basic income. Those layers would have to cover the people and the years this one cannot.

## One rubric for every form

Use the same questions in every form-specific section. Do not collapse them into a single score.

| Question | What it asks |
| --- | --- |
| Addition | Is the claim on top of the wage, or a substitute for it? |
| Access | Who is eligible? Are part-time, contract, and gig workers outside? |
| Usability | When can the household spend it: during a wage shock, or only at exit? |
| Business survival | Can the firm finance the arrangement in a bad year? |
| Persistence | Can the structure be sold out from under the employees? |
| Coverage | What share of workers does this form actually reach? |
| Covariance | How tightly does the return move with the worker’s own wage? |
| Voice | What governance rights attach, separate from the money? |
| Leakage | What do fees, debt service, taxes, and lockups take between the plan and the household? |

Usability and covariance are the two columns that decide whether a form can answer a mid-career wage loss. A ranking of forms by covariance is a proposition to test, not a result: cash profit sharing may be cut first in a downturn; equity value falls with the firm; cooperative pay can be more flexible while jobs are stickier; trust distributions depend on the document.

## Five rules for the draft

1. Separate ownership from compensation. Equity, employee stock ownership plan benefits, and cooperative membership are not the same as a profit-sharing bonus, a revenue-sharing contract, or phantom equity. Compare the rights each one actually gives.
2. Follow the money to the employee. Trace who pays for the claim, how it is allocated, and what fees, debt, vesting, liquidity, taxes, and payout rules do to it. Potential wealth is not cash the employee can spend.
3. Test the counterfactual. Compare equivalent resources paid as wages, as a cash bonus, or as an ownership claim. Include the employer’s cost, the employee’s taxes, the timing, the median, and the downside.
4. Treat adoption and coverage as separate problems. A form can work inside the firms that use it and still reach few workers. Ask who is covered, who is left out, and whether the arrangement adds to pay or replaces it.
5. Treat public policy as help with a price. Ask whether tax advantages, technical assistance, feasibility grants, and financing support create additional adoption and a real employee benefit relative to their public cost.

## Three scenarios

The paper tests three paths. It does not assume that automation raises the value of employee ownership.

**The firm captures the gain.** AI raises productivity or supports a new product, and the business keeps enough of that value as profit or enterprise value. Employees whose arrangement gives them a right to that value may share in it.

**Competition passes the gain to customers.** Costs fall, and much of the benefit shows up as a lower price. The firm can be more productive without a matching rise in profit or equity value. Employee ownership cannot distribute a gain the business does not keep.

**The firm is disrupted.** Demand shifts, staffing falls, or the business loses to a competitor. Employees may face lower wages, less work, and a smaller ownership stake in the same firm.

Employee ownership can align people with a firm’s upside. It also ties their finances to that firm’s fortunes.

## What stays provisional until the draft cites it

- About 11 million active participants in 2023 is an approximate coverage comparison with a workforce on the order of 160 million. The dates and the definitions of “active” and of the workforce have to match. It is not a count of everyone with any form of employee ownership.
- Total plan assets and employer stock are different numbers. Neither is a description of cooperatives, options, or profit sharing.
- “Paid at exit” applies to the employee stock ownership plans whose rules pay at separation or retirement. It is not a description of cash profit sharing, patronage dividends, or every equity award.
- The $2 million appropriation, the count of state programs, and the bills each need a primary citation. Authorization, appropriation, Senate passage, House passage, and money actually delivered stay separate. S.2458 and S.2461 were introduced on 24 July 2025 and have not moved past committee referral. S.1728 and S.2403 passed the Senate on 10 October 2025 and are not law until the House acts.
- The insurance gap is this: existing research has not established that employee ownership reliably insures workers against AI-driven wage or job loss. A systematic review has to say how broadly that sentence can be made. Evidence about wealth or firm performance is not evidence about offsetting those losses. A stylized simulation that offsets about 5 percentage points of value added under expanded employee ownership is a conditional illustration. It is not the paper’s quantitative anchor, and it is not a measured effect.

## Source rules

Advocacy and industry publications are cited as sector material, not as independent causal evidence. Every empirical claim names its population. No mean is reported without the subset. Correlational estimates are labeled as such, including the ones favorable to employee ownership. Bills are dated and described as introduced. An appropriation is not a disbursement. Where a median is unavailable, the paper says so and does not substitute a mean. Model replies are leads. They are not sources. A sentence in the paper maps to a primary document, with a note on whether the result is causal, associational, a simulation, an administrative count, or sector material. The paper discloses assistance from language models in preparing the research design.

Three literature leads stay outside the findings until the papers are opened. One argument is that artificial intelligence can weaken a firm’s reason to offer equity if workers become less scarce. Another is that ownership may change which technologies a firm adopts, not only who receives the gains. A third is mandatory profit sharing, including the French evidence, as the alternative to a voluntary business-led path. The paper can explain why it does not adopt a mandate. It does not cite those papers until they are in hand.

## What counts

Three rights are scored separately.

**Ownership** is a direct or beneficial claim on the firm’s equity or surplus. Employee stock ownership plans, worker cooperatives, employee ownership trusts, and broad-based shares are in this group when the employee actually holds that claim.

**Income-sharing** is a contract for part of profit, revenue, or a measured gain. It can raise pay and still end with the job. Cash profit sharing, revenue sharing, and gainsharing are in this group. A retirement plan that the tax code calls a profit-sharing plan does not have to be funded out of the company’s profits. A cash bonus calculated on profit is a different instrument.

**Participation** is a voice in governance or in how the work is done. Cooperatives include it by design. Many stock plans do not. Financial participation and a say in the firm are related, and they are not the same result.

Three outcomes are reported separately: cash that can be spent now, wealth that may vest later, and what remains if the job and the firm fail together. An employee stock ownership plan balance can grow for years and still pay nothing in the month wages fall. Phantom stock and stock appreciation rights are contracts for cash tied to value unless a share is delivered.

## How common it is

In Department of Labor filings for plan year 2023, analyzed by the National Center for Employee Ownership and updated in January 2026, there were 6,609 employee stock ownership plans at 6,411 companies. They covered 15.1 million participants, of whom 11.0 million were active employees. That active count is the “about 11 million” in this baseline. It comes from the active-participant column of that filing table. It is not the separate Rutgers estimate, in an April 2024 brief using earlier data, that about 11 million employees participate in restricted stock, stock options, and purchase plans that are not employee stock ownership plans. Those two counts must not be swapped. Total plan assets in the 2023 filings were $2.06 trillion, which is over $2 trillion and under $2.1 trillion. Employer stock inside those plans was $398 billion. Most of the asset total sits in public-company plans and is not company stock. About 11 million active participants in these plans is a small share of a U.S. workforce on the order of 160 million. A broader survey estimate, about 18 percent of employees or about 25 million workers with any employer stock, options, or purchase plan, is a different and larger definition. The paper shows both and names which one it is using.

Most of the evidence is about partial stakes. Findings about a minority employee stock ownership plan do not automatically describe a firm that is 100 percent employee-owned. The paper says which results apply to full employee ownership, because that is a different structure and it is the structure the author’s firm works on.

Worker cooperatives are much smaller. The Department of Labor’s report to Congress, citing the 2025 worker-cooperative census, identifies 820 known worker cooperatives and democratic workplaces, about 13,520 workers, and about $806 million in combined revenue. A 2025 state-of-the-sector estimate puts the broader field near 1,300 firms and 16,000 workers.

## What the evidence can support

These are associations and institutional facts. They are not proof that adopting a plan causes the result, unless a study’s design supports that sentence. The literature also contains modest favorable associations: firm performance, worker wealth, and job stability in some past recessions. Those sentences enter the paper only with the study, the years, and the label “association” or “limited to the period studied.” A meta-analysis often cited for a small positive performance link (O’Boyle and coauthors) and the recession studies of Kurtulus and Kruse are leads to open before any coefficient is quoted. They are not yet quoted here.

Workers ages 28 to 34 in the National Longitudinal Survey of Youth 1997, interviewed in 2013, who were employee-owners had higher median household net wealth ($28,500 versus $14,831), higher median wage income ($40,000 versus $30,000), and longer median job tenure (5.2 years versus 3.4 years) than workers who were not. The 2017 report of those figures says the comparison is not a causal estimate. The 92 percent wealth gap is a ratio of those two medians for young households. It is not a large account balance, and it is not evidence about a typical employee of any age.

In French worker cooperatives, when sales run 10 percent below trend, employment falls by about 6.2 percent, compared with about 7.8 percent in conventional firms. Pay also falls by more in the cooperatives. Employment stability and pay flexibility belong in the same sentence.

Research on shared capitalism finds better workplace outcomes more often when a financial stake sits alongside employee participation, training, and a real wage. Ownership by itself is not the finding. In the samples studied by Buchele, Kruse, Rodgers, and Scharf, company stock was not a substitute for pay or benefits. That does not settle the question for profit sharing, options, or every plan.

Almost no study reviewed so far tests these arrangements as insurance against an automation shock. The sentence the paper is allowed to write, until a systematic review is done, is narrower: existing research has not established that employee ownership reliably insures workers against AI-driven wage or job loss. Evidence about wealth or firm performance is not that insurance result. Firms that share ownership may already have been better-managed firms. That selection problem stays in the paper.

## Trade-offs the draft will argue, not assume

A claim on the employing firm ties wealth to the same place as the wage. If the firm automates, shrinks, or fails, both can fall together. A share of revenue can pay cash sooner and still leave no stake, and it can pay out when the firm is not earning. Cooperatives add a vote and are harder to finance at scale. Equity can compound and remain locked until the worker leaves. Plans that cover only some employees, or only firms that choose them, leave everyone else out.

The comparison that keeps the paper honest is an equal-resource one. Take the same economic resources and pay them as higher wages, as a bonus, or as a contribution to an ownership claim. Report the median, the downside, and whether the money can be spent. An average account in a small subset of public-company plans is not the typical worker. In the much larger set of public-company plans combined with 401(k)s, average employer stock was about $18,000 in the 2022 filings used by Blasi and Kruse, not the roughly $125,000 mean in 99 stand-alone public plans.

## What can be done now

Owners decide at two moments: when the firm is founded, and when it is sold. A sale to the people who work there is a business choice beside a sale to an outside buyer. The paper states that opportunity without an uncounted number of firms.

Government can make the transaction easier to understand, finance, and complete. It can also police valuation, because overpayment for employer stock harms the employees the plan is for. The explanatory statement for the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2026 (H.R. 7148, Public Law 119-75, signed 3 February 2026) provides $2 million for Employee Ownership Initiative grants from within the Dislocated Worker National Reserve Demonstration Authority, as described in Senate Report 119-55. That is an allocation inside an existing training account. It is not evidence that grants have been paid to businesses. As of December 2025, the Department of Labor’s state page said at least eight states had state-funded employee-ownership initiatives, and its report to Congress said nine. The count depends on that definition: state-funded initiatives, not every statute that mentions employee ownership.

S.2458, the Employee Ownership Financing Act, was introduced in the Senate on 24 July 2025 by Senator Sanders and referred to the Committee on Health, Education, Labor, and Pensions. It would create an office and a loan program. It is not law. S.2461, the Promotion and Expansion of Private Employee Ownership Act of 2025, was introduced the same day and referred to the Committee on Finance. It would amend the tax code and the Small Business Act for S-corporation employee stock ownership plans. It is not law. Cite each bill for its own subject. Do not describe either as the bills the Senate passed in October 2025.

On 10 October 2025 the Senate passed, by unanimous consent, S.1728, the Employee Ownership Representation Act of 2025, and S.2403, the Retire Through Ownership Act. Both had been reported by the HELP Committee in July 2025. They then went to the House. Senate passage is not enactment. H.R. 3105, also titled the Promotion and Expansion of Private Employee Ownership Act of 2025, was introduced in the House on 30 April 2025 and referred to Ways and Means, Small Business, and Education and Workforce. It is the House bill on S-corporation employee stock ownership plans. It is not law.

Of the $2.06 trillion in total plan assets in the 2023 filings, about $1.69 trillion is in public-company plans and about $363 billion is in private-company plans. Employer stock in private-company plans is about $239 billion. Founder-led transitions of the kind this paper discusses sit in that private-company universe, not in the headline asset total.

Any tax incentive in the paper is tested with one question: does it create an additional claim for employees, or does it pay the seller, the lender, or the adviser for a deal that would have happened anyway?

## Where this layer stops

Employee ownership captures an automation gain only when that employer captures it. If the value sits in a firm with few workers, if competition turns the gain into a lower price, or if a technology supplier takes the surplus, there is little for the plan to distribute. A worker displaced before vesting, a worker never hired, and a worker at a firm that never shares ownership are outside this mechanism. A firm that automates away its own workforce also shrinks the set of people who hold the claim.

About 11 million active participants in employee stock ownership plans cannot be described as coverage of the workforce, and that comparison is approximate until the workforce definition and year are paired with the 2023 filings. For those plans, much of the value is wealth paid at separation or retirement, not income in the month wages fall. That sentence is about those plans. It is not a description of cash profit sharing, patronage dividends, or every equity award. A $12,000-a-year illustration for about 260 million adults, roughly $3 trillion a year, is a stress test of a national cash floor. It is not a proposal of this paper, and it is not a dividend a fund of that size can pay on day one. Federal, state, and community funds, and a cash floor, are the layers that would have to cover those gaps. This paper names the gaps. It does not design those layers.

## What would weaken this account

The draft is weaker if plans systematically replace wages with a riskier payment, if the people who receive claims were already the secure workers, or if under the paper’s own scenarios the claim rarely becomes spendable when wages fall. Those tests stay open. They are the reason the conclusion stays conditional.
